Emotional Reality Before Explanation: Conscious Feeling and Relevance for How to Proceed

Download manuscript PDF →Download companion PDF →

Abstract

A consciously felt emotion can be present while its cause, meaning, fittingness, justification, appropriate response, or behavioral weight remains uncertain. This article develops a cross-literature identification architecture that keeps phenomenal occurrence separate from explicit self-interpretation, third-party interpretation, correctness or accuracy, justification, behavioral authority, and causal provenance. The architecture composes established work on emotion processing, awareness, appraisal, feelings-as-information, emotion rationality and fittingness, and validation; it does not claim those component distinctions as discoveries or propose a new mechanism or latent variable. Its scientific function is to prevent evidence for felt occurrence from silently substituting for evidence about these other relations. Paper-level originality remains open, and the architecture should contract if it adds no distinct identification work.

In plain language

Someone can feel fear, anger, hurt, distress, or simply that something is wrong without yet knowing why. The absence of a good explanation does not erase the experience; it means that the explanation is a separate question that remains unresolved.

The paper keeps several questions apart: whether a feeling is consciously present, what caused it, whether the person’s interpretation is accurate, whether another person understands it, what the feeling justifies, and how much influence it should have over later behavior.

Why it matters

Ordinary conversation often collapses those questions. Statements such as “that does not make sense” can move from disputing an explanation to implicitly disputing the existence of the experience itself. Scientific measurement can make the same mistake when evidence for one relation is treated as evidence for all of them.

Separating occurrence from interpretation protects both directions of inference. A person can be wrong about why they feel something without being wrong that they feel it, and a third party can correctly understand external facts without thereby having direct access to the other person’s experience.

The framework is not a new theory of emotion. Its purpose is to make the evidential stopping point explicit so that felt reality, explanation, correctness, justification, and behavioral authority are not silently substituted for one another.
