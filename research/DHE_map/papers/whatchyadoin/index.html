WhatchyaDoin? Separating Intention, Action Realization, Causal Provenance, and Awareness

In plain language

Wanting to do something, knowing how to do it, and being able to do it do not guarantee that the action actually starts. When an action finally begins, the fact that it happened does not, by itself, explain what caused the transition.

This paper also separates performing an action from recognizing that you are performing it. The moment you notice yourself doing something may not be the moment the action began, and awareness of acting does not automatically identify what initiated the action.

Abstract

People can want to act, know what to do, and remain capable of doing it while the action still does not occur. When action finally begins, it is tempting to treat onset as evidence for a favored internal mechanism, and to treat awareness of acting as evidence about what caused the onset. This paper argues that those substitutions are not licensed. Prior frameworks already decompose intention, action selection and timing, inhibition, and the prospective and retrospective sources of agency. The proposed contribution is narrower: a cross-literature identification architecture that keeps intention evidence, action-specific realization, causal-provenance evidence, and awareness/origin evidence from substituting for one another. Behavioral realization is defined by a predeclared action-specific criterion, while the causal route to that criterion remains a separate empirical question. Changes in realization can occur without a manipulation supplying a new external action identity, but that observation alone cannot establish that latent internal action content remained unchanged. Likewise, explicit awareness may precede, accompany, or follow portions of action, but any downstream causal role requires awareness-specific evidence rather than a generic monitoring effect. The framework does not propose a new mechanism or universal stage model; it specifies what observations do and do not identify when 'I want to do this' becomes 'I am doing this.'

Why it matters

Statements such as “I wanted to,” “I chose to,” “I started,” and “I knew I was doing it” can refer to different observations. Treating them as interchangeable can turn a visible action into an unsupported conclusion about intention, neural mechanism, or awareness.

The proposed framework asks researchers to specify an action-specific criterion for realization, independently examine the causal route producing it, and distinguish awareness of the action from its initiation. It proposes an identification discipline, not a universal sequence or newly discovered mechanism.
