Knowledge Handoffs Under Change: A Structured Assessment Design for Adaptive Revision

Download manuscript PDF →Download companion PDF →
Brain and body systems

DLPFC, aPFC/frontopolar cortex, posterior parietal cortex, dACC, anterior insula, frontostriatal systems

In plain language

Advice is compressed knowledge. A sentence such as “do not configure it that way” can transmit the answer while leaving behind the failures, comparisons, assumptions, and conditions that made the answer useful.

The proposed experiment first makes sure everyone can perform the familiar task. It then changes what information is included in a fixed-size handoff and tests whether recipients can preserve the inherited rule when irrelevant details change, stop using it when the case leaves its intended domain, and re-derive an action when the underlying reason changes.

Abstract

People often pass one another answers that took much longer to learn than they take to say. A warning can fit in a sentence; the observations, comparisons, and conditions that made the warning useful may not. Someone can hear the advice, believe it, and follow it correctly while the situation stays familiar, yet still be poorly prepared to recognize when the advice no longer applies or when the reason behind it has changed. This paper asks what has to survive when knowledge is passed from one person or system to another so that the recipient can benefit from what was learned without becoming unable to judge when the inherited answer no longer fits. Correct routine performance cannot answer that question by itself. The proposed experiment first establishes common competence at a familiar task, then varies what a fixed-dose handoff contains and tests held-out cases that require keeping a rule under irrelevant change, withholding it when the case leaves its declared domain, or re-deriving an action when a parameter in the functional relation supporting the rule changes. A separate evidence-strength control checks the simplest always-preserve or always-revise strategies. The design is explicitly bounded by established transfer, preparation-for-future-learning, Cognitive Flexibility Theory, and Double Treatment assessment logic; it does not claim a new learning mechanism, acquisition-by-assessment principle, or proprietary profile model. The remaining handoff-specific composition is open and should contract if simpler established designs, scoring schemes, or models are equally informative.

Why it matters

Routine success can hide a difference that only becomes visible when the situation changes. Two people can look equally competent while one has enough structure to recognize the boundary of a rule and the other has learned only the answer.

That matters anywhere knowledge is handed off between people or systems: engineering, training, healthcare, families, organizations, and automated decision support. A handoff can be short and still be useful, but brevity can also strip away the information needed for adaptive revision.

The paper does not propose a new learning mechanism. It proposes an assessment design for asking whether a handoff preserves enough usable structure for a recipient to know when to keep, limit, or change what they were told.
